Having had a glance at the vacuum pressure on our ion gauge, I turned around and saw something unusual on the screen. In all our previous runs, the photon counts remained stable, even when the oven source was running. Suddenly, it was steeply hiking upwards. I felt excited, but skeptical. I must have expressed my surprise, because now Johannes came around the table, curious as well. We decided to chop our repumping laser. Immediately, the signal was modulated in a clean binary manner. High signal, low signal, high signal, low signal, etc. And it was still growing. We turned the oven off.

**Ions!** 

After months of aligning lasers, checking the connections of the resonator providing our RF voltage and the DC connections, verifying that our laser frequencies are correct, heating our Barium source over and over and over again, there they are! We were just about to start a week-long deep search, but the ions were quicker. The Ba+ experiment at the LaserLaB VU Amsterdam had first signal at about 10:30 on Thursday, 25 June 2026.

After appropriate celebration on Thursday afternoon, we managed to laser-cool some ions down and crystalize. Here's a picture of the two-ion crystal we were able to capture, just a day after we saw the first cloud:

You might be wondering what is the big deal about this. But for Johannes, Laura and me this is **the** big milestone we have been working towards since the beginning of the experiment. I started my PhD in August 2024, knowing almost nothing about atomic physics or the entire ion trapping business. Since then, together with the Ba+ team, I have been working on setting up our ion trap experiment with the goal of ultimately using quantum entanglement for precision spectroscopy. Using quantum entangled states we want to impose better bounds on potential dark matter candidates and improve ion-based optical atomic clocks. For this purpose, we are building this beautiful experiment from nothing.

Since I joined almost two years ago, the Physics department of the VU, us included, has moved into the new Research building, which means we had the pleasure to build a new setup in a brand new lab. While Johannes worked mainly on the imaging setup and all the experimental control hardware and software (the eyes and brain as I like to call these parts), my work, and the work of our master student, focused on designing and building the laser setup (the heart of the experiment).

The peak of this development phase was the installation of the trap end of October last year. A few months after that the vacuum chamber was baked out and all the optics were installed. In February of this year we started searching for fluorescence from neutral Barium. When that failed, because our photomultiplier tube turned out insensitive to the wavelength in question, we turned to ions. From then on it has been an emotional roller coaster; imagine finding a reason why you failed on Monday, fixing it by Wednesday and confirming by Friday that you still don't know why your experiment doesn't work.

But finally, the search is over. And before we continue tackling the mountain of work that is waiting for us, let's just look at the beauty of this experiment. Single atoms, an electron stripped away, held in space in the middle of our ultra-high vacuum. With a few lasers we make them shine.
